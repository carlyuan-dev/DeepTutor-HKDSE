Sample ENG-01: A repair afternoon, not a replacement for recycling
Subject: English Language. Genre: school newsletter article.
Original practice material; not an official examination paper.

Harbour School is trying a monthly repair afternoon. Students bring broken pencil cases, loose buttons and damaged book covers to the library. Volunteers show them simple repairs. Electrical appliances and sharp tools are excluded because the school cannot supervise specialist repairs safely.

During the first session, 24 students brought 30 items. Eighteen items were repaired, seven needed spare parts, and five could not be repaired safely. These figures describe one afternoon; they do not show how much waste the whole school produces each year.

The organisers initially planned to buy a large tool kit. After speaking to participants, they decided to borrow basic equipment and spend their small budget on thread, patches and replacement zips instead. A teacher said the aim was not to keep every object forever, but to help students consider repair before replacement.

Some students complained that repairs took longer than buying new things. The club accepted this concern and began offering short demonstrations during lunch. Students could then decide whether to return for a longer session. Participation remained voluntary.

The organisers will record which items are still usable a month later. They will also ask students who did not attend why they stayed away. The next decision will therefore draw on more than enthusiastic comments from volunteers.

Reading notes ENG-02
The initiative encourages repair before replacement; it does not replace recycling or guarantee that all items are repairable. The first-session repair proportion is 18/30 = 60%. Long-term waste reduction cannot be inferred from this alone. Follow-up checks assess durability, while feedback from non-participants may reveal barriers that volunteer feedback misses. Distinguish an explicit fact from an inference and qualify claims when evidence is limited.
